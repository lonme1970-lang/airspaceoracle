Execution & Ground Truth / Planning perspective
Airspace Is More Than a Control Measure
A clear overlay is only the beginning. Explore the responsibilities, coordination points, and fallback decisions that connect an airspace plan to execution.
Core question
What should planners understand about connecting airspace plans to real-time execution?
The planning problem
A diagram can show where activity is intended to occur without explaining who makes a decision when the situation changes. This perspective asks planners to connect geometry with responsibilities, timing, and communication rather than treating the graphic as the finished product.
An illustrative scenario
A fictional training event includes several airspace users. A weather change affects the proposed sequence. Instead of simply redrawing a boundary, the team works through who needs to be informed, what must be reassessed, and who can authorize the next step.
Questions to bring to the table
  • Who is responsible for coordinating each activity and communicating a change?
  • Which assumptions about timing, weather, and communications need confirmation?
  • What requires a new coordination or approval decision?
  • How will the team record unresolved issues and communicate the final plan?
Practical takeaway
Ask for a coordination and decision picture alongside the geometry. A useful planning product should make responsibilities and open questions visible.

Capability Integration / Planning perspective
Demonstration Is Not Integration
A successful demonstration answers a narrow question. Explore what still needs to be understood about people, procedures, coordination, and sustainment.
Core question
What questions should an organization ask before treating a demonstrated capability as operationally integrated?
The planning problem
A demonstration can be useful evidence without answering every question about routine use. The planning challenge is to identify what the event actually tested, what support it depended on, and which assumptions remain open.
An illustrative scenario
A fictional team sees a capability perform during a short, well-supported event. Before extending the operating period, it lists the specialist support, connectivity, power, and coordination arrangements that made the demonstration possible.
Questions to bring to the table
  • What was demonstrated, and under which conditions?
  • Which people, procedures, and support arrangements were essential?
  • What happens if a supporting assumption no longer holds?
  • What evidence is still needed before making a broader readiness claim?
Practical takeaway
Separate demonstration results from integration assumptions. A short evidence-and-dependencies register is a practical next discussion product.

Civil-Military Integration / Planning perspective
Shared Airspace Starts with Shared Understanding
Different organizations bring different responsibilities to the same airspace. Start with shared assumptions, understandable requests, and clear coordination.
Core question
How do planners establish coordination across organizations with fundamentally different responsibilities?
The planning problem
A request that is clear to one organization may be incomplete or ambiguous to another. A useful starting point is to explain the proposed activity in terms that each stakeholder can assess, while leaving approval decisions with the responsible authorities.
An illustrative scenario
A fictional training organizer proposes activity near another airspace user. The teams compare time windows, requested geometry, points of contact, and how they will communicate a change before finalizing a request.
Questions to bring to the table
  • Who needs to understand or assess the proposed activity?
  • Are location, time, altitude references, and purpose unambiguous?
  • Which responsibilities and decision authorities remain with each organization?
  • How are changes, cancellations, and unanswered questions communicated?
Practical takeaway
Shared understanding does not require identical missions. It requires a request that others can interpret and a coordination process they can follow.

Operational Sustainment / Planning perspective
Before We Add Another System, How Will We Power It?
Power belongs in the planning conversation—not at the end of it. Ask how loads, charging, fuel, maintenance, and backup affect the operating concept.
Core question
What power and sustainment questions belong in capability planning from the beginning?
The planning problem
The operating concept should explain how equipment remains supported for the intended activity. This is an operational planning discussion: identify loads and dependencies, record assumptions, and ask qualified specialists to validate the technical solution.
An illustrative scenario
A fictional team plans a full-day activity after a short demonstration. It discovers that charging, backup, transport, and responsibility for replenishment were not included in the original schedule. Those become explicit planning questions rather than assumed support.
Questions to bring to the table
  • Which loads, peak demands, duty cycles, and operating periods need verification?
  • Who owns charging, fuel, transport, maintenance, and backup arrangements?
  • Which environmental or equipment constraints need specialist review?
  • What measurements and engineering checks are required before selecting equipment?
Practical takeaway
Make power and sustainment dependencies visible early. Keep engineering design, equipment sizing, installation, and safety validation with appropriately qualified specialists.

Workforce Development / Planning perspective
From ATC to Airspace: Bridging the Planning Gap
Build on control experience while expanding the planning picture. Connect airspace users, coordination responsibilities, timing, and decision-making.
Core question
What additional knowledge prepares an ATC professional for brigade, division, and field-army responsibilities?
The planning problem
This perspective builds on the training gap identified in my work: moving from an ATC background toward intermediate airspace planning. The aim is not to diminish control experience, but to connect it with a wider set of users, responsibilities, and planning decisions.
An illustrative scenario
In a fictional workshop, participants receive a simple activity description and sketch the airspace users, information needs, decision owners, and coordination sequence. The exercise emphasizes explaining assumptions rather than reproducing a particular template.
Questions to bring to the table
  • Can the learner explain the difference between a control task and a planning decision?
  • Can the learner identify affected users and coordination responsibilities?
  • Can the learner communicate assumptions, timing, and unresolved requirements?
  • Can an exercise assess judgment and explanation, not just template completion?
Practical takeaway
Teach the transition explicitly: explain the broader problem, practice a coordination task, and review the reasoning behind the proposed plan.

Doctrinal Architecture / Planning perspective
Why I Developed TALAS: The Problem Before the Framework
Begin with the planning problem, not the acronym. Explore the questions behind TALAS without treating a conceptual framework as validated doctrine.
Core question
What operational problem motivated the concept, and what can be understood without exposing sensitive details?
The planning problem
TALAS is presented here as an author-developed conceptual framework. The starting point is how to make competing airspace requirements, allocation options, and coordination responsibilities easier to understand. A public explanation should distinguish those questions from claims of technical or operational maturity.
An illustrative scenario
A fictional planning exercise introduces competing activities with overlapping time windows. Participants compare allocation options and explain the assumptions, decision responsibilities, and unresolved questions behind each option.
Questions to bring to the table
  • What operational requirement is the proposed allocation intended to address?
  • Which users, assumptions, and coordination dependencies are included?
  • Who makes the decisions, and which questions remain unresolved?
  • What review and evidence would be needed before any practical use?
Practical takeaway
Explain the problem before promoting the framework. TALAS is not presented as adopted doctrine, independently validated performance, or operational approval.
